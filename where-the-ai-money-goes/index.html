AI venture funding, 2024–2026

Where AI venture dollars go vs. where strategy predicts durable profit

What strategy predicts, what six prominent venture firms say, and where the money in their rounds goes

By Rod Feuer · November 2026

Key findings

  • VCs may have few ways to invest in AI's most defensible businesses. In these rounds, startups building systems of record—the category Salesforce, SAP, and ServiceNow already own—got only 0.2% of the dollars.
  • Agents had the most deals, but frontier labs got the most dollars. Agent startups were 44% of the deals and got 7% of the dollars. Frontier labs were 3% of the deals and got 70%, and OpenAI's $122B round alone is about a third of all the dollars.
  • Strategy predicts durable profit goes to chipmakers, hyperscalers, and systems of record, yet the dollars in these rounds mostly went elsewhere. Even compute startups, mostly inference platforms that rent chips, got only 2.5%.
  • Two likely reasons so few venture dollars reached the companies strategy expects to profit: 1) incumbents don't raise venture rounds, and 2) few systems-of-record startups raised rounds at all. The data can't yet say which matters more.
  • A startup's way into records has two routes, and both start with an agent doing the work. In the first, the agent writes its own record of that work, which is still a small bet. In the second, the agent acts on records other systems hold; the latter is likely riskier because the incumbents control access to those records.

The data include 597 AI rounds of $5M or more with a publicly reported size, $357B in all, that six VC firms joined from January 2024 to October 2026. There is no market baseline.

Venture capitalists publish a steady stream of essays telling founders where AI will create lasting value. A firm's checkbook is a more honest record of its beliefs than its blog. And even when the words and the checks agree, the firm can still be wrong.

To test both, we collected every AI funding round that six prominent firms took part in from January 2024 through October 2026: Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), Sequoia Capital, Foundation Capital, Menlo Ventures, Greylock, and Bessemer Venture Partners. Counting rounds of $5 million or more with a public size, that comes to 597 deals worth $356.6 billion, and one of the six firms led 329 of them. We set those rounds against the firms' published theses, and against established competitive strategy on where AI's durable profits should end up. The strategist Hamilton Helmer calls those profits persistent differential returns.

Strategy predicts that durable profit goes to three groups: chipmakers (Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom), hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle), and the systems of record, with their rules, that hold a company's data. The frontier labs that supply the models hold only a lead against open models that they must renew with each new model release. Five of the six firms write mainly about apps and agents. Strategy expects those companies to keep their margins only for a while. The deals lean the way the essays do, with 58% in apps and agents, while the dollars go elsewhere, with 70% in frontier labs. One round, OpenAI's $122 billion, is about a third of all the dollars. Systems of record, in the strongest group in strategy's reading, get $638 million, about 0.2%.

Fowler: AI makes custom code cheap, except where everyone shares one version

Chad Fowler's essay "SaaS Isn't Dead. Sameness Is." gives the frame. Fowler, a partner at BlueYard Capital and a former chief technology officer of Wunderlist, starts from one observation: AI makes custom code cheap. Companies used to buy the same software as everyone else because building their own cost too much. If AI writes much of that code, the case for identical applications weakens, though software sold as a service survives.

Fowler borrows Stewart Brand's idea of pace layers. A building has parts that change quickly, like furniture and paint, and parts that change slowly, like the foundation. Software has both kinds, and Fowler's advice fits in one line: "Standardize the slow layers. Generate the fast layers."

The fast layers sit closest to the user: the screens people look at and the agents that do tasks. Each team can now generate these apps and agents for itself, cheaply and often.

The slow layers sit underneath. They hold the rules a business runs on, the systems of record it treats as the official truth, such as Salesforce's customer database or the accounting ledger, and outside data no single company owns, such as payment networks. A company can't run on five different ledgers, so everyone shares one version of these records and rules. Fowler draws a line between the two groups, which we call the contract line.

Salesforce sits on both sides of that line. Its customer database sits below it. Its seat-priced screens and configured workflows sit above it, where AI makes them easier to replace. Salesforce's future depends on the first part, and its risk comes from the second. Its recent moves toward usage-based pricing and a fixed-fee agent license fit that prediction. Fowler invests in the slow layers his essay favors, so he has a stake in the answer.

Fowler's stack leaves out the models and the chips they run on

Fowler's stack leaves out the AI model and the chips it runs on, and those suppliers have bargaining power of their own. We added them as a third group, models and compute. It holds frontier labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic, specialized labs such as ElevenLabs in voice, vendors of training data, and the chips, data centers, and energy everything runs on. A fourth group, other, holds robotics, biotech, and defense, whose companies tend to own their whole stack.

Inside each group the data is finer. Apps and agents covers several layers:

  • Interfaces, labeled UI in the charts (Perplexity, Gamma).
  • Agents (Cursor, Harvey, Sierra).
  • The harnesses that run agents (LangChain, n8n).
  • Derived context layers, which build a picture of a company from data held in other systems (Glean; "derived context" in the charts).
  • AI-native services firms that do the work and answer for the result (Crosby).

Records and rules covers systems of record (Rillet), tools that test and police AI (Braintrust), data platforms such as Databricks, and external data. Figure 1 lists every layer. We sorted each company into one layer, by how it mainly earned money at the time of its round. The questions we asked, in order, are in "How we did this."

Strategy predicts durable profit goes to chips, hyperscalers, and systems of record, while frontier labs hold a lead they must renew

In our framework's reading, durable profit goes to systems of record, chipmakers, and the hyperscalers. GPU clouds and energy suppliers earn more only while supply is short, and inference platforms, which rent chips to run other companies' models, hold at most a temporary advantage, a window. Frontier labs hold a lead on the newest models, which they must renew with each release.

Our framework for judging which layers can keep their profits starts with Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers. Helmer argues that a business earns durable profits only if it has a Power, an advantage competitors can't copy or compete away. Two of his seven matter most here. Switching costs are what a customer loses by leaving, like the pain of moving a company's books to a new accounting system. Scale economies mean a big player's costs per unit are lower than a small one's. The other five are network effects, counter-positioning, branding, a cornered resource such as exclusive data, and process power.

Helmer's test is strict: anything a well-funded rival can copy within a year or two won't protect profits. Being "AI-native" is not a Power. Neither is generating software faster, or even sitting in a slow layer.

We read each layer through four more lenses: Clayton Christensen on how profit shifts as products become good enough, Michael Porter on industry structure, Brian Arthur on increasing returns, and Avinash Dixit on commitments and how rivals respond. One AI model applied all five, so their agreement counts for less than five experts' would. The method note at the end explains how. The predictions are dated November 3, 2026, and this article doesn't test them. Some readings came on November 21, after the deal counts were known: the frontier-lab lead, the narrower reading of compute, the derived-context layer, and three types of product in Figure 3.

Three findings bear on the lab reading. Epoch AI finds that since January 2026 the best open-weight models have lagged the best closed models by about four months on its Epoch Capabilities Index (May 2026). Epoch AI also estimates that the cheapest price for a given level of capability has fallen about 13-fold a year on average, across all models, open and closed (September 2026). The rate slows as a level ages, to about 4.7-fold a year two years on, and Epoch finds the fiercest price competition near the frontier has been among closed-weight models. Ramez Naam's November 2026 analysis of OpenAI data finds AI speeds its researchers by 3.6-4.4% per capability point, well below the 15-19% he says a self-improving loop needs.

Products that span layers rarely do better. Only two types rate strong: a system of record that owns its rules, and one built on licensed data. The data shows no clear case of either.

1Apps and agents can't keep their profits for long

All five lenses agree that an app or agent company, on its own, gets at best a temporary advantage, a window. Its one Power is what Helmer calls counter-positioning: a newcomer adopts a business model the incumbent can't copy without damaging its own business. Older software companies charge per seat. An agent company can charge for the work done, such as each contract drafted or each support ticket closed. An incumbent that matched that price would cut into its own seat revenue, so for a while it holds back, and the newcomer wins customers.

Counter-positioning fades as incumbents adapt, and some already are: Salesforce now sells its AI agents under a fixed-fee license. It also fades as other startups copy the pricing. Unusually reliable agents might earn a second Power, process power, though none has shown it yet.

An app or agent company has one way out: becoming the place where a customer's own records live. That gives it switching costs, and at that point it has moved into records and rules. The usual method is to keep a record as a by-product of the work. An agent that drafts contracts or approves expenses can log what it saw, what it decided, which policy applied, and who signed off. Over time the customer relies on that history for audits and precedent, and leaving would mean losing it. An index of a company's documents builds no such lock, because anyone with the documents can rebuild it. Incumbents push back by adding the same features to their own systems. Large customers also negotiate the right to take their data with them, but an exported decision record is raw data. The links to the rules that applied, and the structure that makes the history usable, stay behind. Foundation Capital's case for decision records rests on this bet.

The framework doesn't say how long the window lasts. A window of several years can return a venture investor's whole fund, so this conclusion doesn't by itself make the firms' agent bets a mistake.

2The strongest position is records plus the rules that govern them

All five lenses agree that the strongest position is a system of record that also owns the rules applied to it: who can approve a payment, which customer data can be shared, which checks an AI's output must pass. A customer who leaves such a company has to rebuild its rules as well as move its data. The pairing also explains why rules are weak on their own. A rival can copy the wording of a policy, but it can't copy the system that enforces it. The Power is switching costs.

Four of the five lenses also rate external data feeding a system of record as strong, as long as a license or regulation restricts access to that data. A payment network is the clearest case, because joining one takes approvals and partners. Freely available data adds nothing a competitor can't copy.

The case rests on two assumptions: that moving off a system of record stays hard, and that the record keeps mattering. Bessemer argues leaving gets easier, because AI cuts the cost of migrating and can rebuild recent records by capturing them as work happens. a16z and Menlo argue the records stay put but lose their value to the agents that act on them.

3The labs hold supplier power, and can move up into apps and agents

Four of the five lenses give the labs and compute providers what Porter calls supplier power over every app and agent: the power to set the terms. A handful of frontier labs supply the intelligence that every agent and app runs on, so they can raise prices, change access, and keep more of the gains. That is power to bargain today. Whether the labs' profits last is the separate question of Power, which depends on how hard they compete with each other. Records and rules feel this less. A system of record also uses models, but its value lies in its data, and it can swap one model for another. The exception is testing and policing AI, where the labs now sell tools of their own.

Multi-model routing limits the labs' pricing power, but it does not stop them from entering apps and agents. Agent companies build to switch between models, and the best work across several labs. Prices for general-purpose models have been falling. The labs can build agents and apps themselves, three of the lenses expect them to, and OpenAI, Anthropic, and Mistral already do.

Outside critics doubt the labs' margins will last. Gary Marcus wrote in November 2026 that Anthropic "doesn't have much of a technical moat" and is "constantly forced to drop prices." Benedict Evans wrote in July 2026 that "every dynamic we can see" points to the labs becoming "low-margin commodity infrastructure providers," though he adds that no one can know yet. Anthropic's own prospectus, filed for its November 2026 listing, warns that "the degree of differentiation among advanced AI models may narrow."

Its results so far cut against Marcus's claim that it cuts prices "faster than it cuts costs," at least for the cost of serving customers. Revenue less that cost rose from 29% to 86% of revenue between the first nine months of 2025 and of 2026, while revenue grew from $2.5 billion to $33.2 billion. The figure appears to leave out the cloud marketplaces' cut, and it leaves out the computing used for training. Nine months of results can't show whether that margin lasts. Research spending still left an operating loss.

The firms back the labs in the largest rounds they join, which puts their money behind the companies most likely to compete with their agent bets. Those labs also take most of the round dollars in this data.

4Adding an interface brings no new Power, except for the labs

All five lenses agree that the interface, the screens people look at, is the easiest layer to copy. AI now generates screens cheaply, a small share of builders can flood the supply, and an agent can copy an app on request. An interface can also hurt. Apps are usually priced per user, which clashes with pricing per transaction or per outcome in the stronger layer. A records company that builds its own apps also starts competing with the partners who build apps on its data. In the detailed ratings, adding an interface weakens half of the twelve layers it could be paired with, including all four layers of records and rules, and strengthens none outright.

The clearest exception is a lab that owns the product its users see as well as the model underneath. OpenAI builds the model and sells it through ChatGPT, its own app. Specialized labs such as ElevenLabs in voice and Suno in music also sell mainly through their own apps. Their Power would depend on training data that is licensed or exclusive. For a lab, adding an app moves profit toward the lab and puts pressure on independent apps.

Ben Thompson's aggregation theory makes the strongest case for the lab exception. A company that becomes users' first stop can treat its suppliers as interchangeable and keep most of the margin, as Google did with websites. If ChatGPT becomes that first stop, OpenAI would not need its model to stay unique. Being the first stop lasts only if something keeps users there. The lock the theory points to is the user's own history: the memory, files, and past work an assistant builds up. That history works like a personal system of record, which brings the argument back to switching costs.

Users can easily keep several assistants at once, which weakens the aggregation case, because being first is not the same as being exclusive. Google, Apple, and Microsoft already own first stops, in search, phones, and office software, and are building assistants into them. Each AI query also costs real computing power, while aggregation works best when serving one more user costs almost nothing. Those costs are falling, but they are not yet close to zero. The test is simple to state: whether users stay with ChatGPT when a rival's model is clearly better.

The lenses split on agents that keep records and apps that build models

The lenses disagree on whether an agent should keep its own system of record. Christensen and Arthur say to integrate now. The technology is too young to split cleanly, so the company that controls more of it learns fastest. Dixit and Porter say not to, because moving into records provokes the incumbents and turns partners into rivals. Helmer says the combination has Power only if the company holds the decision record.

An app or agent that runs its own specialized model gets a conditional verdict: it pays where the data is exclusive or general models fall short. Helmer and Porter call it a cost without Power unless the model rests on data rivals can't buy.

Most of the firms say profit moves to apps and agents

Five of the six firms reach the opposite answer from our framework. The framework expects durable profit to go to the chipmakers, the hyperscalers, and the record holders. Most of the firms argue it will go to the apps and agents built on top of them, the layer the framework gives only a window.

Their most common argument comes from Christensen. When one part of a product becomes good enough and cheap, profit moves to the part next to it that is still hard to get right. As models become cheap and interchangeable, profit moves up to agents and industry-specific applications. Bessemer's 2025 report put it in one line: "Vertical AI is the new SaaS." We read what each firm published about AI over the period and counted how many of its theses make each of five arguments.

The Christensen argument leads at a16z, Sequoia, Foundation Capital, and Bessemer, and ties for the lead at Greylock. Menlo Ventures is the outlier. Its most common argument is that AI will let companies sell finished work instead of software tools. Its 2025 writing also argues that spending is gathering around a few frontier models, and its enterprise report notes that "Anthropic now earns 40% of enterprise LLM spend." Foundation adds a twist: it bets that agents win by producing a records-and-rules asset, the decision record.

The two views are closer than they look. Christensen's argument is about where profit moves next, and our framework's is about where it can stay. A firm can believe agents will make money for the next five years and also believe that advantage will fade, and venture capital is built to profit from such windows. The firms rarely say in public that the window will close.

Nearly six in ten deals go to apps and agents

Count the deals, and they lean the way the essays do. Nearly six in ten deals are in apps and agents. Agent companies alone are 43.7% of all deals, and each firm sits between 36% and 57%.

Apps and agents are 58.0% of the six firms' AI deals but 7.9% of the round dollars. Models and compute are 18.1% of the deals and 74.7% of the round dollars.

Agent companies are 43.4% of the 597 deals, and between 36% and 57% of the deals at each of the six firms.

Agent companies are not simply young companies caught by early investing. If they were, agents would make up more of the early-stage deals than the later ones, yet agents are 44.3% of early-stage deals and 43.3% of later ones. Who chose the deal doesn't matter either: agents are 43.8% of the deals the firms led and 43.7% of the deals they only joined.

The same pattern fits firms that simply invest in whatever startups are raising money. The share holds whichever way the deals are cut, and a market full of agent startups would produce that too. Without a count of every AI round in the wider market, we can't say whether 44% is a deliberate tilt or a mirror of how many agent startups exist. A rough test across the six firms found only a loose link between how much a firm writes the Christensen argument and how many agent deals it does.

Of the 329 led rounds, 61 (19%) went to a company coded as building a second layer, and about a third did if the uncertain cases are counted. The count shows what companies build. It doesn't show that building across layers pays off.

Nearly seven in ten dollars go to frontier labs

Count the dollars, and the picture flips. Apps and agents get only 7.9% of the money. Agents, at 43.7% of deals, get 6.8%. Models and compute get 74.7%, almost all of it in frontier labs, which are 2.8% of deals and 69.7% of dollars. Compute draws 36 deals and $8.9 billion, 2.5% of the money, most of it in inference platforms such as Baseten and Fireworks AI. Seven frontier-lab rounds went to labs that also sell their own app, and those seven hold $226.6 billion, 63.6% of all the dollars.

Frontier labs are 2.8% of the 597 deals and 69.7% of round dollars, and OpenAI's $122 billion round alone is about a third of all round dollars. Systems of record are 19 deals and 0.2% of round dollars.

Part of the frontier labs' share is arithmetic: OpenAI's single $122 billion round is about a third of all the dollars in the 597 deals. Frontier labs raise enormous rounds even early: among seed and Series A rounds, they are 1.7% of deals and 32.8% of the dollars.

A firm that joined a $122 billion round may have written a small share of it. Dollar share shows which layers need huge amounts of capital, and says less about what the firms believe.

Three of the four lab backers put most of their lab dollars in rounds they didn't lead

Four of the firms hold frontier-lab rounds, and three of them mostly joined without leading. Sequoia joined 72% of its frontier-lab dollars without leading, including Anthropic's $30 billion Series G, though it co-led Anthropic's $65 billion Series H. Menlo and Bessemer mostly went along in Anthropic's rounds. a16z is the exception: 85% of its frontier-lab dollars sit in rounds it led or co-led, mainly OpenAI's $122 billion round and Thinking Machines. Greylock and Foundation hold no frontier-lab rounds at all.

Firms that call models a commodity still back the labs, though less than the dollars suggest: only 6 of the 329 deals they led were frontier labs.

  • Menlo's lab rounds match its own research, which says enterprise spending is gathering around a few frontier labs. Its money sits mostly in Anthropic.
  • Sequoia and Bessemer mostly joined lab rounds without leading them. That fits a hedged bet: if the labs end up squeezing the agent companies, the lab stakes gain what the agent bets lose. The lead role in the largest rounds often goes to giant strategic investors anyway.
  • a16z co-led OpenAI's $122 billion round and led Thinking Machines. It was one of six co-leads on OpenAI, so its own check may still be small. If this is a real bet, it may be a bet on ChatGPT as the users' first stop, the aggregation case in point 4, more than on the model itself. A firm is also not a single mind: the partners who write the essays are often not the ones who make the largest investments.

Every firm also has plainer reasons. A large fund has to put large sums to work, and one lab round absorbs more capital than dozens of startup rounds. In venture capital a few huge winners pay for everything else, so a firm can doubt a company's margins and still want to own part of it. Without the firms' own check sizes, the data can't say how much of each firm's money these bets represent, or which motive drove them.

Capital-heavy layers raise a few giant rounds, and agents raise many small ones

The firms do lead model companies when the models are specialized. Specialized labs are 9.4% of led deals and 2.3% of dollars: firms will back a model for voice or chemistry, and rarely lead a bet on a general-purpose lab. Greylock bets on a different part of this group. Baseten and Modular, which serve and run models, make up 38.2% of its round dollars, which looks like a bet that durable profit lies in running models more than in building them.

Set the frontier labs aside, and robotics, agents, and data infrastructure each take about a fifth of the remaining money, in different ways. Waymo's single $16 billion round is 60.9% of all robotics dollars, and one Databricks round is 49.5% of data infrastructure. The largest agent round, Cursor's $2.3 billion raise, is only 9.6% of the agent dollars. Capital-heavy industries collect money through a few giant rounds, and agents collect it through hundreds of smaller bets.

Without the frontier labs, robotics, agents, and data infrastructure each take roughly a fifth to a quarter of the remaining round dollars. One Waymo round is 61% of robotics dollars, one Databricks round is about half of data infrastructure, and agents' dollars come from hundreds of rounds, the largest under a tenth of the total.

AI-native services show the clearest gap between words and money

All six firms published arguments that AI will let companies sell finished work, 29 such arguments in total. Across all six firms we found only 18 services deals, worth about $658 million, about 0.2% of the dollars. Counting only the firms that plainly do the work themselves, the figure is 10 deals and about $452 million, 0.13% of the dollars. Our framework sees a real but temporary advantage for these firms, since an incumbent that bills by the hour can't easily switch to billing by outcome. In this data, the theses run ahead of the money, unless most such deals fall below our $5 million floor.

Records and rules, the strongest group, get the least money

Our framework rates records and rules the strongest group in the chain. Leave out data platforms, and systems of record, rules, and external data together draw $3.5 billion across 75 deals, about one dollar in every hundred. Models and compute draw $266 billion, roughly seventy-five times as much. The group's headline share of 6.7% comes almost entirely from Databricks, whose rounds make up $20.0 billion of the group's $23.8 billion.

Systems of record, the framework's single most attractive layer, get 19 deals and $638 million, 0.2% of the money. The largest round is Rillet's $100 million. Counting second layers too, systems of record get 26 deals and $1.5 billion, 0.4% of the money, mostly in rounds for EliseAI and Serval. No deal shows clear evidence of the strongest combination, records plus the rules that govern them, though startups rarely document such features.

Two things probably explain why so few venture dollars reached these layers. First, the incumbents hold the positions and don't raise venture rounds. The largest systems of record belong to Salesforce, SAP, Oracle, and ServiceNow, and the established chipmakers and hyperscalers hold the chips and the cloud. All are public companies or parts of them, so they are largely absent from a venture dataset. Their corporate venture arms invest in startup rounds, but the companies themselves don't raise them, and a few chip startups, such as Etched, did. Second, few records startups raised rounds at all: 19 deals, the largest Rillet's $100 million, against 261 agent deals. At the median, a records round (about $25 million) is close to an agent round (about $30 million), so the gap is in how many deals there were. The frontier-lab average of about $15 billion a round is pulled up by OpenAI's $122 billion. The first reason explains who is missing from the data, not why startups raised so little, and the data can't say which matters more or whether either is the cause. If our framework is right, the strongest position in the chain is one where venture capital has few ways to invest. The firms' own theses are a third possibility: five of the six write mainly about apps and agents.

The firms' agent bets then read as bets against the incumbents who hold that position. a16z, Menlo, and Bessemer say as much in their essays: agents will take the value records hold today. Here the essays and the deals point the same way, against the framework's strongest prediction. Which side is right depends on whether durable profit stays with systems of record or moves to the agents that work on them.

Rules and evaluations show the same pattern from another angle: many led deals, little money. Evaluation companies are 9.1% of the deals the firms led, and Bessemer led all six of its evaluation rounds, yet they draw 0.6% of the dollars. Our framework wouldn't call that underfunding. It gives testing tools little Power unless they are joined to a system of record, so many small bets may be the sensible price.

Two possible routes into records and rules

If records and rules are the strongest group, and incumbents already hold them, a venture-backed startup needs a way in. The firms' essays describe two routes, and both start with an agent doing the work. In the first, the agent keeps its own record of that work. In the second, it works on records someone else holds.

The first route comes in two versions. Bessemer and the growth investor Tidemark, outside this article's six firms, describe a new AI-native system of record that does the work itself, which Bessemer calls a system of action (route 1a in Figure 9). Foundation Capital describes an agent that writes a new kind of record, a record of its decisions (route 1b). The second route leads to what Greylock's Jerry Chen called, as early as 2017, a system of intelligence: the layer that reads the records, reasons over them, and acts, while the records shrink into infrastructure. Foundation argues for the first route and Bessemer leans toward it. a16z and Menlo argue for the second.

1Become a new system of record

Bessemer's version starts from the record. Its system of action is a new AI-native CRM or ledger that does the work and keeps the record in one product, meant to "live alongside incumbent SoRs initially and then slowly replace them." Tidemark describes the same path: start on top of the incumbent, then "pull in or replicate core functionality" from the old record.

Foundation Capital argues that the record an agent leaves as it works, which it calls a "context graph," is the most valuable thing an agent company can own. Of the 10 Foundation essays coded for the asset that protects a company, 8 name this decision record.

Only a context layer that writes something down first, at the moment a decision is made, creates a record no one else has. Many context layers only index data that systems of record already hold, which makes them copies the owner of the source data can rebuild or cut off.

Foundation's idea turns Fowler upside down. Fowler puts durable value in records and rules. Foundation says an agent company can win because, by doing the work, it produces a record no competitor holds. Incumbents can make it harder to pull data out of their systems, but they can't insert themselves into an agent's work after the fact. That holds only while the startup's own agent runs the work. If incumbents' agents or the labs' platforms run it instead, they write the decision record.

The first route is still a small bet. Foundation put $114 million into four companies it cites as building decision records, against $8 million in one company that keeps them as a standalone record. It also put $115.5 million into an AI-native accounting system and a payroll system, records in the older sense, and $79 million into products that read other systems' data without keeping a record. Across all six firms, 11 companies building new AI-native systems of record drew 18 deals and $789 million, about 0.22% of the money. EliseAI, which sits between the routes, would add $600 million. That count misses agent companies that keep a record of their own, and those are hard to spot from outside. In a random draw of 30 agent companies, our coders found five that held their customers' official record, and they agreed on only one that kept a record of its decisions.

2Become the system of intelligence

a16z and Menlo take the second route: the record stays, and the value moves to the agents that act on it. a16z's "From System of Record to System of Intelligence" (May 2026) argues that the CRM "isn't going to go away" but becomes an input, while "the locus of value is migrating upward." A Menlo essay calls systems of record commodity stores of context. Neither needs a startup to become a new system of record, and both need the old record to stop mattering. Sequoia funds companies here, including Rowspace and Edra, two context layers, without a strong public position. EliseAI, which runs agents for housing and healthcare operators, drew $600 million. It sits between the routes. Its agents work with the systems operators already use, but our coders also credit it with records of its own, so Figure 3 counts it as a new system of record that does the work.

A system of intelligence depends on records it doesn't hold, so any rival can read the same records. The Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard for connecting agents to data now backed by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, AWS, Salesforce, and Snowflake, makes that reading easier still. The record holders still decide who may read, and on what terms, and open reading lets the labs and the record holders build the same layer. To keep its profits, a system of intelligence needs its own history of the work, including the rules it applied and what its agent has learned. Building that history is the first route. Without it, the second route may keep its profits only for a window.

A system of intelligence can also be a way into a new system of record. It is the easier place to start, because the customer keeps its records and nothing has to move. Once a startup's agent runs the work, it can begin keeping its own record, though the record holder has reason to narrow access before that record grows. Serval, which sells AI agents for IT service management, may be a case. Coverage of its October 2025 round describes only agents, while its December 2025 announcement calls it "a full system of record for enterprise service management." That may show a change of route, or only a change in how Serval describes itself.

Every route touches the rules, but none clearly owns them. An agent runs under the customer's permissions, and a decision record logs which rule applied. Owning the rules means being the place where a business writes its policies and enforces them, and the firms' essays say little about who will. Menlo comes closest, putting the advantage in a product's hidden business logic. Among the 33 rules-and-evaluations companies, our coders found one that sells business rules, and it fits only in part. The rest sell AI security, tests of AI output, or controls on what agents may do. That last group, agent governance, is the closest thing to a standalone rules business, but it holds rules without records. Security incumbents have already bought two of the AI security companies. Microsoft, which calls Agent 365 "the control plane for AI agents," competes for the same spot.

The labs and the record holders are moving in on the independent agent

An agent company on either route faces pressure from both the labs and the record holders. The evidence below leans toward Dixit and Porter: the independent agent company looks squeezed between the labs and the record holders. The second route looks more exposed.

The labs are moving up into agents. In February 2026 OpenAI launched Frontier, a platform for running and coordinating agents, including agents built on other companies' models, with HP, Intuit, Oracle, State Farm, Thermo Fisher, and Uber among its first customers. How widely it has spread since is not public. Frontier and Microsoft's Agent 365 both look like bids to become the system of intelligence, and they fit what our framework expects.

The record holders are moving in from the other side. Every major system-of-record vendor shipped a context layer of its own between February 2025 and October 2026. ServiceNow, Atlassian, and SAP draw on their own systems, and Atlassian bought Traceloop, which traces what AI systems do. The context-layer startups sold so far went to companies that own records or process data. The first route has to survive this counter-move. MCP leaves the second route exposed too: a product that only reads records holds little a rival can't also read, and the record holders can limit even that. No similar standard exists yet for decision records.

Agent harnesses give startups a real performance edge today, though it may not last. An agent is a model plus the harness around it, the code that gives the model its tools, instructions, and checks. The model comes from a lab, and the harness is what the agent company builds. In one test the same model scored 46% in one harness and 80% in another. On a security benchmark, OpenAI's model scored higher inside Cursor than inside OpenAI's own Codex, though for Anthropic's model the gap was small. The best harnesses also work with several labs' models, which limits the labs' power. A good harness can still be copied, so our framework expects any lead it gives to last only for a window.

A system of intelligence that only reads other companies' records competes head-on with the labs' platforms, and the record holders can limit what it reads. Writing the decision record remains the main opening on either route.

Two ways systems of record could lose their profits

Leaving a system of record could get cheap. Moving a company's books, customer history, or employee records has always been slow, expensive, and risky, and that pain is the switching cost that makes records strong. If AI agents can migrate records cheaply and safely, records lose their main Power, and the incumbents who own them lose much of their protection. This is Bessemer's argument. The evidence so far is one company: Klarna, the payments firm, moved off Salesforce and Workday. Klarna has unusual engineering resources, though, and its own chief executive doubts most firms will follow.

Leaving could also stop mattering. A business could keep its records where they are while the work moves elsewhere. If agents read the records, act on them, and keep their own history of what they did, the old system becomes storage, and storage earns little. This is the case a16z and Menlo make. Switching costs stay high, but there is less worth locking in, and no customer has to leave for the record holders to lose. The test would be whether record holders keep growing revenue from their customers as more of the work runs through agents.

How to keep score

Each prediction comes with a test, and none of the tests uses funding data. Funding shows what investors believe. Margins, customer retention, and pricing, layer by layer, will show whether they were right. Tests 1 to 4 matter most here, and tests 5 to 8 come from the article's own argument. A yes to each question supports the prediction.

  1. Records plus their rules. Do systems of record that enforce their customers' rules keep customers longer than those that only store data?
  2. An agent with its own record. Once incumbents add context layers, do agent companies that write the decision record keep customers better than those that don't?
  3. Pressure from the labs. Do independent agent companies lose share and margin faster in categories a frontier lab enters than in categories the labs leave alone?
  4. The services advantage. Do AI-native services firms earn better margins than rivals that still bill by the hour?
  5. Leaving gets cheap. Do record holders' retention and renewal prices fall faster in categories where AI tools that migrate their data have gone on sale?
  6. Aggregation. Do users stay with ChatGPT after a rival's model beats it in users' blind comparisons?
  7. Leaving stops mattering. Do record holders whose customers move more work to agents grow revenue from those customers more slowly than record holders whose customers move less?
  8. Shutting out the second route. Do record holders whose customers move more work to outside agents restrict or charge for those agents' access more?

None of the numbers these tests need is available yet. The public record holders' revenue from existing customers will show up first, in their own reports, along with the terms they set for outside agents' access. The startups' margins and retention sit inside private companies and may come out slowly.

Figure 10 lists the framework's eleven predictions about pairs of layers. Tests 1 to 4 check four of them, H1, H3, H6, and H11, in that order. Tests 5 to 8 check claims from the article's own argument, which Figure 10 doesn't list.

What this does and does not show

Funding records what investors believe, and it can't show where profit will end up. A firm can also be right about the trend and still pick the wrong companies.

  • Published essays are a selected sample. Firms write partly from conviction and partly to market their own portfolios, and the essays and the largest deals may come from different partners.
  • The dollar figures count the money in the rounds the firms join, not their own checks. One round dominates them: without OpenAI's $122 billion, models and compute fall from 74.7% to 61.6% of the dollars, and apps and agents rise from 7.9% to 12.0%.
  • The data covers venture-backed startups only. The incumbents who hold records and rules, and the labs' own moves up the chain, fall largely outside it.
  • There is no market baseline. The most important missing number is the share of agent companies among all AI rounds, not just these six firms' rounds. We also coded four investors outside the six (Kleiner Perkins, a large generalist; Conviction and AIX Ventures, two AI-focused seed funds; and NVentures, a corporate fund) and kept them out of every figure here. They do not fill the gap. Agents are 36 of Kleiner's 89 rounds, about the same share as in the six firms' rounds. They are 20 of the 64 rounds at the two AI-focused funds, a lower share that is too uncertain to tell apart from the six firms', and 11 of NVentures' 63, clearly lower.
  • The data has known gaps. The a16z and Sequoia counts are floors, three firms were built from a commercial database that we checked against public sources, and 23 round records were hard to place in a single layer. The corrections so far each moved the market-wide figures by about a point or less, far less than the gap between deals and dollars. Small layers move far more. Rillet's $100 million round alone is 15.7% of the systems-of-record dollars, and the November 21 recode moved that layer from 24 deals and $806 million to 19 deals and $638 million.
  • The scorecard has limits of its own. The labs choose which categories to enter, so test 3 can mistake their choice for their effect, and makers of migration tools choose their categories too (test 5). Tests 1 and 5 can pick up the same difference if migration tools mostly target records that lack their own rules.
  • Nothing here shows cause and effect: not that a firm's essays led to its deals, nor that any layer's position caused its profits.

The takeaway

The money in the firms' rounds lands mostly on frontier labs, the supplier our framework rates as strong only for now. Our framework predicts that the apps and agents built on top of the labs and the record holders will compete hard and keep their profits only for a window. The strongest position, records joined to their rules, belongs mostly to incumbents, largely public companies, that fund themselves rather than raise venture rounds. Venture capital cannot buy the incumbents' position; it can only back challengers, and those drew about 0.2% of the money.

The six firms write about apps and agents and place most of their bets there, with agents at four in every ten deals. The money in the rounds they join lands on models and compute, with seven of every ten dollars in a few frontier labs. Most of those lab dollars sit in two rounds that a16z and Sequoia helped lead. Records, rules, and external data together get about one dollar in a hundred.

The agent bets are bets against the incumbents. One route into records and rules is to become a new system of record, either an AI-native CRM or ledger that does the work or an agent that records how each decision was made. It is still a small bet, about 0.22% of the money. The other is to become the system of intelligence that reads the old records and acts on them. It has no separate count, because almost every agent company reads records, and it faces both suppliers at once. Unless it builds a record of its own, our framework expects it to keep its profits only for a window.

One reading is kind to the firms. Venture firms profit from windows, and a window of several years can return a whole fund. The lab rounds fit three plainer motives: a hedge for Sequoia and Bessemer, a large fund's need to put large sums to work, and the chase for the few huge winners that pay for everything else.

A less flattering reading is that the essays describe the part of the market that is easiest to invest in, which may differ from the part most likely to keep its profits.

The data can't settle which reading is right. The scorecard above can, and its first results will appear in the record holders' own reports.

How we did this

The rounds. We collected every AI funding round of $5 million or more, with a public size, that the six firms led or joined from January 2024 through October 2026. a16z, Sequoia, and Foundation were built from public sources and cross-checked against Crunchbase. Menlo, Greylock, and Bessemer were built from Crunchbase exports and checked against primary sources: every round of $50 million or more, every led round, and a sample of the rest. A round two firms both joined counts once.

The layers. Each company got one main layer, by how it mainly earned money at the round date, plus any second layers it builds itself. AI models coded the companies under written rules that we set, and AI models also ran the checks against sources, with no human check of individual rows. The layer codes were revised on November 21, 2026: screens became interfaces, workspaces were removed as a separate layer, read-only derived context got its own layer, and labs that sell mainly through their own app were coded as labs. Every recoded row carries a dated note.

The ladder. The figure below lists the questions in the order we ask them. The first yes decides a company's layer. As a check, a separate AI coder applied the ladder to 33 sampled rounds from the six firms, without seeing our codes. It matched our code on 30 of them. The coder is the same kind of model that made our codes, and for many rows it relied on its own background knowledge, not a fetched source page. So the match is weaker evidence than a human check would be. The three mismatches came where public sources are silent or conflict on what customers mainly pay for.

The framework. We used an LLM to apply five strategy frameworks, Helmer's, Christensen's, Porter's, Arthur's, and Dixit's, to every layer and every pair of layers, on November 3, 2026. Some readings were made on November 21, after the deal counts were known; section 2 lists them. One model produced all five readings, so their agreement is less independent than five separate experts would be.

The coding checks. Two AI coders, working separately from public sources, sorted the companies in the routes section. They agreed on all 11 companies building new AI-native systems of record. Of Foundation's own four examples of decision records, they agreed that three work on other companies' records and split on the fourth. Because both coders are the same kind of model, their agreement is weaker evidence than two people's would be. Each firm's theses were coded by the argument they make; Menlo, Greylock, and Bessemer were coded later, and their counts are less firm.
